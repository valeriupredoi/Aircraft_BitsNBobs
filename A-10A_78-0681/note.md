# A-10A 78-0681 Desert Storm

A-10A 78-0681 "Playtime"/"Little Lady Diane" of 353rd TFS/354th TFW "Panthers", tail code "MB" - Myrtle Beach AFB, SC - King Fahd Airport, Saudi Arabia, piloted by Capt. Paul T. Johnson: the famous rescue mission:

## The rescue of Slate 46 — 21 January 1991

Based primarily on Walter J. Boyne’s article, “Hog Heaven,” in Air Force Magazine, December 2010, pp. 34–38. [Read the original article (PDF)](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/PDF/MagazineArchive/Documents/2010/December%202010/1210hog.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com).

The rescue of a downed F-14 crewman by A-10 pilots during Operation Desert Storm was an extraordinary combat search-and-rescue (CSAR) mission. Captain Paul T. Johnson, flying Sandy 57 (MB 78-0681), led the effort deep into Iraq, locating the survivor despite unreliable navigation, limited fuel and the approach of Iraqi forces. His A-10 flight ultimately destroyed the vehicle threatening the survivor and enabled an MH-53 Pave Low helicopter to pick him up. The mission was remarkable not simply because the rescue succeeded, but because of how far the A-10s penetrated into hostile territory and the coordination required to get the survivor out.

On 21 January 1991, Johnson and his wingman, Captain Randy Goff in Sandy 58, were flying from King Fahd Royal Airport to King Khalid Military City Airport in Saudi Arabia. They had been delayed by weather and were about ten minutes from landing when they received an unexpected CSAR tasking. An F-14, Slate 46 (sse below for details on the Tomcat), had been shot down by an Iraqi surface-to-air missile. Both crewmen had ejected:

**F-14A+ shot down:**

- Lieutenant Devon Jones, the pilot.
- Lieutenant Lawrence R. Slade, the radar intercept officer (RIO).
- F-14A+ BuNo 161430, assigned to VF-103 “Sluggers”, USS Saratoga, callsign AA212.
- TARPS, shot down by SA-2 near Al Asad, pilot rescued, RIO POW
- replaced by BuNo. 161424

Johnson received a tentative set of coordinates, together with the call signs for a KC-10 tanker and the MH-53 Pave Low rescue helicopter. He headed west, refuelled from the tanker and contacted an E-3 AWACS. The AWACS initially diverted him to investigate a suspected Scud missile site. At the time, Scud attacks were a major concern because an Israeli response against Iraq might fracture the coalition fighting to liberate Kuwait. Johnson investigated and confirmed that the suspected site did not contain Scuds. He refuelled again, contacted AWACS and was sent back to the original rescue coordinates.

Johnson now had to locate the survivor using coordinates supplied by an Automatic Direction Finder (ADF). The system was imprecise, and the initial coordinates ultimately proved incorrect. He flew east and picked up Jones’s radio signal, but the ADF needle refused to turn. Johnson then flew back west and swung north, following the signal as it grew stronger. He eventually found himself farther into Iraq than any A-10 had previously flown, west of Al Asad Air Base and approximately 60 miles north of the coordinates he had originally received. Eventually, the ADF needle swung around and pointed towards Jones. Flying beneath the cloud at about 300 feet, Johnson passed directly over the downed pilot but could not see him. Jones had dug a hole in the barren terrain with his survival knife and was well camouflaged inside it. Johnson memorised the surrounding terrain and entered a mark point into his inertial navigation system. Unfortunately, that system had accumulated substantial drift during the five hours he had been flying, so he knew its coordinates were not reliable either. The problem was now more complicated than simply finding Jones. Two Pave Low helicopters, an AWACS, a KC-10 and a flight of F-15s were all participating in the rescue. Radio traffic became so intense that Johnson had to intervene and demand that communications cease except between the survivor, the A-10s and the Pave Lows. The helicopters were also using ADF equipment, but they were too low to pick up Jones’s signal. Johnson was the essential link between the survivor and the rescue helicopter.

Johnson had another problem: he was now beyond bingo fuel, meaning he no longer had enough fuel to return safely to base. The standard rule in combat search and rescue was not to leave a survivor behind. Johnson nevertheless had to leave the immediate search area temporarily to refuel. He told Slate 46 to stay put and turned south towards the KC-10, which had itself flown about 25 miles into Iraqi territory at 15,000 feet. After topping up his fuel, Johnson immediately returned to the search area. He rendezvoused with Moccasin 05, an MH-53 Pave Low flown by Captain Thomas J. Trask, with Major Michael Homan as copilot. Johnson led the helicopter back towards Jones and again obtained an ADF indication that helped identify the survivor's location.

But the situation was deteriorating. While the Americans were trying to pinpoint Jones, an Iraqi military truck approached across the desert, raising a cloud of dust. Johnson still could not see Jones. He therefore asked Slate 46 to describe the truck's position relative to himself so that the A-10s could engage it. Jones replied: “The truck is headed straight for me.” Johnson and Goff rolled in for an attack with their A-10s' 30 mm GAU-8/A cannons. They flew low over the ground, but the first pass missed the truck. They turned sharply and attacked again. On his second pass, Johnson destroyed the vehicle. He later described the scene to William L. Smallwood for the book Warthog: "the truck was burning, with orange flames and black smoke pouring out of it." The rescue helicopter then came in and landed. About 100 yards from the burning truck, Jones jumped up out of his hiding place and ran towards the helicopter. The A-10s had not merely found the survivor; they had eliminated the immediate threat to his escape.

The rescue was not quite over when Jones reached the helicopter. Johnson refuelled for the fourth time that day and escorted the Pave Low until he was released to return to King Fahd. His total combat flying time for the day was eight hours and 45 minutes. The article describes the operation as an A-10 mission of unprecedented length and depth into Iraqi territory. The outcomes for the two F-14 crewmen were very different:

- Lt. Devon Jones: rescued successfully and returned quickly to his ship, USS Saratoga. He had been flying with VF-103, the “Jolly Rogers”.
- Lt. Lawrence R. Slade: captured by Iraqi forces approximately four hours after ejecting. He subsequently appeared on Iraqi television with two other coalition prisoners and was released on 3 March 1991 as part of a group of six POWs.

Captain Johnson received the Air Force Cross for his role in coordinating Jones's rescue. Trask and his MH-53 crew subsequently received the Mackay Trophy for their part in the operation. 78-0681 ia now at the US Air Force Museum, Dayton, Ohio.
