# The Ju-52 in Romania

### Vasile Tudor, *Modelism International*, No. 4, 2000

- translated by ChatGPT and V Predoi from the [original manuscript](https://modelism.ro/articles/2000_04/ju-52-in-romania/).

This transport aircraft was used in Romania between 1932 and 1945, operating with a high degree of reliability. In 1980, some were still flying passengers, representing at least 40 years of service, while a Swiss example was still flying as far as Tokyo in the first months of 2000.

Manufactured by Junkers Flugzeuge, production began in 1930 with the single-engined Ju-52/1m. In less than a year, this had evolved into the three-engined Ju-52/3m, which made its first flight in February 1931. An extremely robust all-metal aircraft, covered in corrugated aluminium sheet to increase lift, nearly 5,000 examples were built. It was initially ordered in Germany by Deutsche Lufthansa, after which it flew on many of the world's airline routes in the service of other airlines in more than 30 countries. It was also used as a bomber during the Spanish Civil War, but its principal roles were transport and airborne operations. It was heavily relied upon when Germany occupied Austria and Czechoslovakia, as well as during the invasions of Norway, Denmark and the Netherlands.

Its most costly operation, however, was the airborne assault on Crete, where large numbers of the aircraft deployed in Greece were lost, either destroyed or damaged. Similarly, during the early stages of the campaign in Soviet Russia, and especially during the encirclement at Stalingrad, Ju-52s formed the backbone of troop resupply. The aircraft also carried out almost the entire air bridge across the Mediterranean in support of German forces fighting in North Africa. This continued until the British air force gained air superiority, since the Ju-52 was only lightly armed.

Several versions were produced throughout the Ju-52/3m's production run. These culminated in the Ju-52/3mg6e, which was fitted with a magnetic ring for detecting naval mines of the same type, followed by the g5e and, later, the g7e. The latter had an increased payload capacity and was also fitted with floats. It was used in Switzerland, Norway, Crete and even the Black Sea. The final versions included the g8e and g4e, whose characteristics were slightly superior to those of the original models. From 1943, Ju-52s were also built in German-occupied France and, after the war, in Spain.

With the end of hostilities, Ju-52s returned to their original role, with some remaining in service with air transport companies for many years.

The first Ju-52 to arrive in Romania was an aircraft powered by Hispano-Suiza inline engines. It was purchased with public support in 1932 by Prince Bibescu, then president of the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale (FAI). Flown by renowned Romanian aviators, he used it for the benefit of this prestigious international organisation, also transporting various delegates to FAI congresses. With only 13 passenger seats, it was eventually transferred to the civil aviation service, where it operated only domestic routes. After being damaged in an accident on 23 May 1940, an attempt was made during its repair to replace its engines with BMW-132s, but this proved unsuccessful, and the aircraft was restored to its original configuration. Apart from this aircraft, no others were purchased before the Second World War, with the exception of one acquired in 1939 from the Polish airline LOT, which had withdrawn to Romania to escape the advancing German forces.

The German trimotor was, however, well known to Ion Antonescu himself, the Romanian head of state. From January 1941, Hitler made his own Ju-52 available to transport Antonescu to various official meetings between the two men in Germany and, later, to another meeting in Poland. In March 1941, Göring also sent him a Ju-52, which flew him to Vienna and brought him back after his meeting with the head of the German air force.

With Romania's entry into the war against the Soviet Union, and especially as the front moved farther away from bases in Romania, the shortage of transport aircraft became increasingly acute, making their purchase necessary. Under order No. 4590 of 22 November 1941, the first six Ju-52/3m aircraft were ordered from Germany. By 16 December 1941, two had arrived, and another 16 were ordered in March 1942. All were powered by nine-cylinder, air-cooled BMW-132 radial engines.

In the meantime, the Aerotransport Group had been established using aircraft requisitioned from the LARES company, and it operated from Băneasa Airport. Following the arrival of the Ju-52s, the 105th and 107th Heavy Transport Squadrons were formed. One was initially detached to Cetatea Albă and subsequently to the airfield at Morozovskaya, in what was then the Soviet Union. From the outset, the group's purpose was to carry out transport missions in support of the front.

Once a sufficient number of aircraft was available for this purpose, the question of training pilots arose. The Aerotransport Group and LARES therefore began training pilots to fly multi-engined aircraft. Under the command of Commander Traian Udrișchi, the first course for the new pilots was organised. Most were flight instructors at civilian flying schools. Among them were Decebal Vasiu, Ștefan Mateescu, Dumitru Radu, Victor Ștefan, Radu Zosim, Aurel Ifrim, Ion Vasile, Aurel Stoenescu and others. The courses were taught by engineer Ștefan Andrei, Max Manolescu and Viorel Niculescu. Flight training was conducted with the assistance of Constantin Cantacuzino, supported by Vasile Ciobanu and Milan Glosan, using Potez 561s, Dragonfly aircraft and Ju-52s.

Because the war had engulfed almost the entire European continent, LARES reduced its airline network in 1942, even though the volume of air transport increased. Instead, the number of flights supporting operational requirements on the Eastern Front and transporting wounded personnel grew. Naturally, these flights required crews to navigate in poor weather, often without adequate technical facilities to ensure flight safety.

The accident of 8 February 1942, near Odesa, is a significant example. The aircraft was a Ju-52/3m registered D-AXVC, retaining the registration with which it had arrived from Germany. It was transporting wounded men from Sarabuz to Tiraspol. The weather was good after departure and throughout the first 250 kilometres, but almost the entire region was subsequently covered in fog. Viorel Niculescu, one of the finest pilots of the period, nevertheless managed to reach the vicinity of Tiraspol, but the dense fog prevented him from landing. He headed towards Nicolaev and then followed the coast towards Odesa. Eventually, the aircraft struck the ground, which was glaringly difficult to see against the snow. The impact tore off one landing-gear leg and severely damaged the aircraft.

The crew suffered a few bruises. The wounded men survived, with the exception of one soldier who died, and all 24 were taken to hospital. It was not considered a failure to prepare the mission adequately. Instead, it was concluded that the airfields sought by the crew lacked radio transmitters and receivers, as well as weather stations staffed by qualified personnel who could be contacted in such critical circumstances. It was for precisely this reason that a LARES technical team was sent to Melitopol on 19 and 20 March to open the airfield there. The team was flown to and from the site by Bazu Cantacuzino, who carried out the required flights in a trimotor to certify the airfield.

From the middle of 1942, Marshal Ion Antonescu began flying in one of the Ju-52s brought from Germany, specially configured and carrying tactical number 9. Its crew consisted of Max Manolescu, a flight officer, as first pilot, and Second Lieutenant Decebal Vasiu as second pilot. They were accompanied by Carol Lörintz as radio operator, Private Constantin Copelea as flight mechanic, and two sergeant-majors serving as onboard gunners. Over 13 calendar days, the aircraft flew for more than 17 hours along the route Bucharest–Nicolaev–Odesa–Poltava–Pavlograd–Simferopol–Odesa–Tiraspol–Bucharest.

With experienced pilots from civil aviation and newer pilots trained by the military, Ju-52s were the only aircraft capable of supporting operational air units and even ground formations, assisted only by German aircraft of the same type. They contributed to the conduct of operations and to strategic withdrawals.

One such mission involved Ju-52 No. 11, which was damaged during ground manoeuvring at the Timocevskaja airfield on 13 August 1942. After unloading the barrels of petrol it had brought, the crew took ten wounded men aboard. While turning at the end of the airfield to line up for take-off, one wheel passed over a mine, which immediately exploded. Apart from one man who suffered a leg injury, the personnel escaped unharmed. The aircraft, however, suffered serious damage, and repairing it required a new wing and other components.

During the Stalingrad campaign, the Romanian air force was to participate with eight fighter squadrons and two bomber squadrons. Subordinate to the Combat Air Group, they were to operate in support of the Romanian Third Army, their principal mission being to support its defensive operations along the Don sector. Once deployed, the squadrons took part in aerial battles and attacks against enemy strongpoints obstructing the advance of opposing forces.

With the arrival of winter, however, the situation changed almost abruptly, and the initiative passed to the opposing side. On 19 November, Soviet forces broke through the front at the Don Bend, forcing German and Romanian troops to retreat or remain encircled, as indeed happened in many cases.

At the request of the Third Army, the Combat Air Group made five Ju-52s belonging to the 105th Transport Squadron available. These aircraft landed at Golovski, bringing ammunition and food to General Lascăr's group, comprising approximately 40,000 men, and to the 1st Armoured Division. Both had been encircled on the evening of 20 November 1942.

This was a courageous undertaking by the transport pilots, who penetrated to the encircled troops with essential supplies while operating almost without fighter escort. As a result, some of the personnel in the retreating columns commanded by General Sion managed to reach the positions of the German 22nd Armoured Division, with which they subsequently broke through the Soviet encirclement.

The 7th Fighter Group was also encircled alongside the ground forces at Karpovka. It managed to escape by taking off and landing at other airfields controlled by Romanian or German aviation. The situation was more difficult for the aircrew who had been caught there without their aircraft, together with technical personnel and ground troops, all of whom remained trapped inside the encirclement.

Faced with this situation, the Combat Air Group command began urgent preparations to evacuate them by air. Although Captain Constantin Abeles had only a few Ju-52s available, at 08:05 on 23 November two aircraft took off towards the encircled personnel. The German liaison officer boarded the aircraft piloted by Răduț Zosim.

At take-off, the cloud ceiling—the height of the cloud base—was 200–300 metres. As the aircraft approached the Don, it fell to 100 metres, and fog subsequently appeared. These conditions forced the crew of the second transport aircraft to turn back.

Zosim Răduț, a civil aviation pilot, penetrated the fog and reached or approached Karpovka, then fired the signal flares that constituted the day's recognition code. The personnel on the ground responded with their own flares, indicating the landing direction.

After the manoeuvre, the aircraft was stormed by ground troops within minutes. The air force officers, led by C. Rozariu, intervened to establish who would embark and who would leave aboard the newly arrived aircraft. Order was eventually restored after the initial rush, and the transport aircraft was loaded immediately. Soviet tanks were firing at extremely low trajectories, and several shells exploded less than 100 metres from the aircraft as it began to move.

It took off as quickly as possible, with shells bursting ever closer. Fortunately, at an altitude of approximately 50 metres, the aircraft entered the clouds. Only then did the crew discover that the German officer had been wounded by one of the 90 bullets of various calibres fired from the ground, which had perforated the trimotor.

Shortly before the aircraft took off, one of the most desperate incidents occurred. Although the soldiers had been ordered to return to their defensive positions and await the arrival of further aircraft, two men—who feared that Soviet captivity would be among the worst possible fates—clung to the aircraft. They balanced themselves on the struts supporting the horizontal tailplane, one facing forward in the direction of flight and the other facing backwards.

This happened during the bitter Russian cold at the end of November. The man facing forward fell not long after take-off and was killed when he hit the ground. The other, who was holding on with his back towards the direction of flight, remained there for approximately 150 kilometres, exposed to the full force of the slipstream. When the aircraft landed, he was found frozen stiff and was so firmly stuck to the strut that they could barely detach him. He was taken to hospital and, miraculously, survived.

Another Ju-52 transport pilot was Leonida Tăruș, born on 26 July 1911 in Breanova, Orhei district, Bessarabia. During the resupply of the forces at Stalingrad, he demonstrated great courage, flying in all weather conditions because he was thoroughly familiar with instrument flying.

During the winter of 1942–1943, he transported fuel and food to the 1st Armoured Division, which was trapped at Osinovski. He landed under heavy Soviet artillery fire, with the guns only three kilometres away. Tăruș and his comrades carried out these genuinely sacrificial missions in extremely difficult conditions, flying over the densest network of enemy anti-aircraft guns.

During one such mission, enemy anti-aircraft fire severely damaged his rudder and its control rod. He was forced to rely on all his professional skill to return safely to his departure base, once again demonstrating the remarkable qualities of the trimotor.

The evacuation of the personnel of the 7th Fighter Group continued over the following days. Throughout the defensive fighting along the Don, the 105th Heavy Transport Squadron, commanded by Captain Constantin Abeles, carried out heroic work. Its efforts culminated between 22 and 26 November 1942, when, despite extremely unfavourable weather and considerable risks, its aircraft flew behind enemy lines and transported 43,200 litres of petrol, 33,500 kilograms of food, 3.8 tonnes of ammunition and 511 wounded men.

For these achievements, the squadron and its commander were commended in Daily Order No. 59 of 16 December 1942.

These extraordinary wartime efforts were accompanied by a series of losses, some particularly painful. During January and February 1943, one Ju-52 was lost and another became unavailable for an extended period at Rostov. One of the serious accidents suffered by the 105th Heavy Transport Squadron occurred on 24 February 1943, near the edge of the airfield at Melitopol. Three Ju-52s took off from Nicolaev that day, carrying petrol and oil in metal drums for the 7th Fighter Group, which was at the front.

The formation was supposed to be led by Second Lieutenant Decebal Vasiu, reserve officer. However, reserve aviation warrant officer Victor Ghiță took off first in aircraft No. 5, followed by the other aircraft.

Although there was no threat of interception by enemy fighters on the route to Melitopol, the trimotor pilots, whose aircraft carried no weapons, almost instinctively tightened their formation. As they approached the airfield, Ghiță made a sudden turn towards the inside of the formation without warning. His aircraft struck the wing of aircraft No. 8.

Despite having its right wing broken, Vasiu reduced power on the centre and left engines while setting the engine on the shortened wing to full throttle. With the controls pushed fully to the left, he managed to recover the aircraft from its spin, bringing it out in a wide, descending spiral before landing at speed on the designated airfield. This was a relatively rare occurrence, demonstrating not only the pilot's skill but also the aircraft's stability.

On 30 March 1943, it was the turn of aircraft No. 19, German serial number 7518, belonging to the 107th Heavy Transport Squadron, to be involved in an accident. Piloted by Sever Dumitriu, who had an impressive number of flying hours, including 475 on the Ju-52, it crashed immediately after take-off, three kilometres east of Slobodka railway station in Râbnița district.

The aircraft had delivered 800 litres of petrol for a German aircraft that had run out of fuel while transporting part of the 43rd Fighter Squadron's pilots. Although the terrain was very uneven, Dumitriu managed to land on a strip of ground not far from the German aircraft. After unloading the petrol, he prepared to take off again from the soft ground.

To bring the engines to maximum power, the pilot used overboost. However, he failed to disengage it after the aircraft lifted off. In these circumstances, the engines began to vibrate and misfire, overheating and losing revolutions. This led to an emergency landing in which the aircraft first touched down on its left wing, then on the right landing-gear leg, damaging approximately 60–65 per cent of the airframe.

The fuselage broke into two pieces, the centre wing twisted, the landing-gear attachments became deformed, the engine mounts and propellers were damaged, the flaps bent and the tailwheel broke off. The Germans immediately took possession of the aircraft, but it proved impossible to replace it with another aircraft as they had agreed.

In April, two Ju-52s were detached to Kerch in Crimea, at the disposal of Colonel Gărbea's detachment, which was responsible for organising the evacuation of troops from the northern Caucasus and Kuban region.

One of the pilots was Decebal Vasiu. For an extended period, he carried out several transport flights a day from Armavir to Krasnobar, and as many as eight flights a day from Vernikovskaja, Anapa and Anapskaja, which were closer to the evacuation area. These hurried evacuations took place under the constant threat of Soviet fighters and bombers, which were also effective at night.

Despite the losses and difficulties, considerable hopes were placed in these transport aircraft. It was proposed that some should be adapted for parachute training and others for towing military transport gliders. Eventually, one or two were used by parachute troops, while IAR-39s were employed to tow DFS-230 gliders, having been fitted with the necessary hooks for attaching and releasing the tow cable.

One Ju-52 also went to Ziliștea for radio training for future night-fighter pilots being trained there. The aircraft were indeed in heavy demand, and on 20 May another six were ordered from Germany, in the hope of filling the gaps in the fleet.

Meanwhile, the Germans repaired the aircraft damaged at Melitopol, fitting it with a wing taken from another aircraft damaged near Tighina. It was immediately requested by the Romanian Air Corps, to which a transport detachment equipped with Ju-52s had been attached.

Eventually, Ju-52 No. 8 was sent to Băneasa in Bucharest because it was considered to require a complete overhaul following its accident. Ju-52s Nos. 4, 6 and 21 were also directed there. Among their pilots and crew were Second Lieutenant Decebal Vasiu, Warrant Officer Nicolae Pompei, flight officer Nicolae Cristescu and aviation warrant officer Gh. Chiriac.

Ju-52s continued to supply operational units of the Romanian Air Corps and other military formations from all branches of service operating on the fronts at Izium, Mius and Kuibyshev. They also supported the strategic withdrawal during the summer and autumn of 1943 and the first months of 1944.

They continued operating in support of forces in Crimea, despite aircraft being damaged and lost. One example was aircraft No. 17, which was caught by Soviet aircraft at the Leprtiha airfield and set on fire. Aircraft No. 21 was shot down over the Black Sea on 24 January 1944.

This led to renewed efforts to fit the aircraft with a 13.2 mm machine gun. However, none could be found in the depots, as they had been installed on IAR-81 aircraft. Later, an attempt was made to adapt a gun taken from a downed American aircraft, and a prototype was assigned to the Cugir factory for manufacture.

Although the entire country was at war and human losses were accepted with grim resignation, one of the most painful accidents in the Ju-52's Romanian career occurred on 14 February 1944. Piloted by aviation warrant officer Gh. Șerbănescu, the aircraft crashed near Tighina in bad weather, killing an entire artistic troupe being transported to perform for troops at the front.

Romanian aircraft had also helped bring home as many people as possible during the withdrawal of German and Romanian forces from Crimea, particularly wounded personnel.

From February 1944, as the retreat from the Eastern Front intensified, extending from Nicolaev to Odesa and then to Tecuci, the Romanian Air Corps relied primarily on Ju-52s Nos. 4 and 6, assisted by Nos. 1, 7 and 19 from Cetatea Albă. These aircraft transported personnel and supplies.

In March 1944, when the 9th Fighter Group and the assault aviation units moved from Nicolaev to Odesa, operating from the airfields at Dalnic and Tatarka, almost the entire burden fell on five Ju-52s. On 15 March, Ju-52s Nos. 1 and 28 transported the aircrew of the 3rd Stuka Group from Odesa to Karamkul after the men had spent leave in Romania.

From 29 March, four aircraft—Nos. 1, 6, 27 and 28—also participated in the airlift back to Romanian airfields, assisted by several transport gliders. The crews of all the aircraft that took part in the Romanian Army's withdrawal from the anti-Soviet front performed their missions with great dedication.

Unfortunately, they experienced a disastrous period in which almost all the trimotor transports were lost within a single week. Two were lost following serious pilot errors on 4 and 7 June, while four were destroyed by fire or strafing by American aircraft at Botoșani airfield on 10 June 1944. Among them, aircraft Nos. 9 and 20 had been specially configured and used by Marshal Antonescu, while one aircraft, registered YR-ALN, belonged to the ARES company.

As if these losses were not enough, further Ju-52s were lost following the political and military events that took place after 23 August 1944. Aircraft Nos. 14 and 28 were burned at Băneasa Airport during fighting with German troops attempting to seize the airfield. Aircraft Nos. 4 and 18 were then shot down by enemy aircraft on 31 August 1944.

As a further consequence of these events, Ju-52 No. 27, which had been sent to a hangar to transport materials needed by the aeronautical industry, never returned to Romania and was probably captured. Another aircraft sent to Prague for repairs also failed to return.

Two other Ju-52s were captured by the Germans at the airfields at Ziliștea and Matca. To complete the account of losses during this period, mention must also be made of the aircraft shot down by the Soviets near Sibiu on 4 September 1944, killing its entire crew. Nor should the aircraft burned by the Germans at Giarmata-Timișoara on 15 September 1944 be omitted.

It is true that several aircraft were captured from the Germans after 23 August, including some Ju-52s, but very few were airworthy. Some, such as those found at Fierbinți, Snagov and Târgoviște, were severely damaged, and only a few spare parts could be recovered.

Another aircraft, found at Alexandria and in somewhat better condition, had its fuselage cut open by Soviet soldiers so that they could use it as a shelter. By contrast, the aircraft captured at Turda became airworthy after being fitted with other engines and having its bullet-riddled fuselage repaired. It received tactical number 100.

Two other captured aircraft, numbered 33 and 34, were also repaired. Together with the aircraft carrying numbers 30 and 35, they were sent to the Western Front for a short period, where they were urgently needed. Before long, however, they were handed over to the Soviet Union and taken to Moscow, after the pilots sent from there had received instruction in flying them in Bucharest.

As a result of the events of 1942–1944, an inventory drawn up on 1 January 1945 showed that military aviation had only 11 Ju-52s left, of which just six were operational. Civil aviation still had five trimotors, three of them operational: YR-ALO, YR-ALM and YR-ALL.

With fewer aircraft available and no longer any need to disperse them, both the Aerotransport Group and the 105th Heavy Transport Squadron were based at Giulești from January 1945. They attempted to assemble a mixed transport squadron that could be sent to the front as quickly as possible. Ultimately, they were able to send only Ju-52 No. 25.

According to Daily Order No. 26 of 26 February, this aircraft had been attacked a few days earlier by three German Bf 109s while carrying out a transport mission on the Lucenec–Miskolc–Oradea route. Thanks to the skill of its gunners and, above all, that of Captain Lazăr Popescu, who was piloting it, the crew managed to land safely on an improvised airstrip, saving both the aircraft and its occupants.

The Romanian Army's systematic dismantling continued. In accordance with a directive from the Allied Control Commission in Romania, the 105th Heavy Transport Squadron and the 109th Glider Transport Squadron ceased operations at 20:00 on 1 March 1945. The establishment of a Mixed Transport Squadron to operate at the front, subordinate to the Specialised Base, demonstrates that the measure had been planned.

After the war, alongside many military pilots, several transport aviation personnel were awarded the Order of Aeronautical Virtue. They included Zosim Răduț, Decebal Vasiu, Dumitru Radu, Ștefan Petre and Al. Ionescu, as well as radio operator S. Grigorescu and aircraft mechanic Dumitru Dumitrache. Their contribution to supplying the Romanian Army at the front with materials, fuel and ammunition had been exceptional.

But what happened to Romania's Ju-52s?

Following the reorganisation of military aviation after the end of hostilities, and in accordance with Air Staff Order No. 2883 of 13 August 1945, the 7th Air Transport Flotilla was established at Giulești, near Bucharest, on 20 August. It was equipped with Ju-52s, Me 108s, Fi 156s and other aircraft.

Ju-52 No. 7, which had departed from there, was involved in an accident at the royal airfield at Văcărești on 19 July 1946. The aircraft was piloted by Lieutenant Commander Constantin Stoian. The brake-reduction valve on the left wheel momentarily jammed, causing the aircraft to swing around and damaging the landing gear.

On 26 July 1946, the military aviation inventory contained three serviceable and three unserviceable Ju-52s. Aircraft No. 31 had previously been transferred to civil aviation and was subsequently placed at the disposal of the Royal Household.

Civil aviation had a similar situation: three serviceable and two unserviceable aircraft. The former had been transferred to TARS, the Romanian-Soviet Air Transport Company. They were considered to have originated with the former LARES company, and TARS made little use of them before they returned to civil aviation.

The two unserviceable aircraft were not taken over and were eventually withdrawn from service. One of the aircraft used by TARS, piloted by Gh. Grozea, crashed into the mountains of the former Czechoslovakia while operating the Bucharest–Prague route in exceptionally bad weather.

A particularly interesting episode occurred at the beginning of 1947, when the Royal Household requested the approval of the Allied Control Commission—which in Romania was then exclusively Soviet-controlled—to borrow temporarily one of the better military Ju-52s, No. 1 or No. 9. King Michael wanted to use it for a trip abroad.

Although the aircraft was to be piloted by Captain Traian Udusch and the Allied Commission approved the request, the King apparently abandoned the plan in the end. At the same time, measures were taken to repaint Romanian national markings on aircraft used by the Royal Household, including two U-2s received from Stalin and the Soviet government, a Fi 156, two Fw 58s and aircraft No. 31.

In 1947, several pilots succeeded in leaving Romania using military and civil aircraft. To prevent further aerial escapes, strict orders were issued requiring the Aviation Division to concentrate all multi-engined aircraft, including the five Ju-52s, at Giulești airfield. Their magnetos were to be removed and their cockpit access doors locked pending further orders.

As the restrictions became increasingly severe, the Aerotransport Group, which controlled these aircraft, requested permission a month and a half later to stop draining the remaining oil and petrol, as required by the original order. Instead, it proposed removing the throttle levers, starter switches and some of the operating controls.

The group also asked that the fuel and oil no longer be drained, arguing that the procedure was impractical and jeopardised the proper functioning of the aircraft. It considered stronger security measures sufficient. Nevertheless, the orders remained in force for a long time.

Despite these increasingly stringent restrictions, Ju-52s continued to perform the missions required of them. On 7 October, one transported Me 109 pilots to Brașov, from where they were to fly to Craiova the first such aircraft delivered by IAR after the war.

Meanwhile, one civil aviation aircraft was being prepared to spray agricultural land with insecticide, and plans were made to manufacture a 1,500-litre dusting or spraying apparatus.

By February 1948, the Ju-52 situation had changed little. Aircraft Nos. 1, 3, 11 and 15 were at Giulești, while No. 24 was at Băneasa, detached to the Presidency of the Council of Ministers, which was already trying to establish its own flight unit.

Ju-52 No. 31 was also at Băneasa, assigned to the Ministry of Internal Affairs. It appears that its crew subsequently used it to leave the country.

With fewer transport aircraft available, military aviation tried to obtain one or two from civil aviation, offering spare parts in exchange. On 7 February 1949, civil aviation offered the airframe registered YR-ALK. The military rejected it, whereupon YR-ALM was offered instead. This aircraft was also considered unsuitable, particularly because its horizontal tailplane had been struck by a glider.

Determined to obtain an aircraft, the military requested YR-ALO, despite the fact that it had been built in 1943 and had accumulated 1,705 flying hours over 1,822 flights. It was being used for sport parachute operations, however, and ultimately no agreement was reached.

Although their available aircraft were insufficient, military aviation managed to find the resources to participate in the air parades held on 1 May and 20 December, using three Ju-52s alongside other aircraft. Both military and civil aviation struggled to retain the aircraft they still possessed, particularly as the aircraft belonging to the Presidency of the Council of Ministers eventually returned to military aviation.

In 1951, Romanian sporting aviation imported two Ju-52s from the former Czechoslovakia, again for use in parachute-jumping operations.

Both military and civil Ju-52s continued flying for several more years, fully demonstrating their usefulness. Their cabins had long been regarded as exceptionally well equipped. We shall therefore conclude this brief review of the distinguished career of the Ju-52 in Romanian service, an aircraft whose performance impressed its contemporaries.

On 13–14 April 1953, a commission of Romanian and Soviet military specialists carried out the alignment and calibration of the goniometers at the radar stations at Deveselu, Craiova, Călărași, Caransebeș, Ciacova and Șepreuș, in accordance with the CFAM plan.

For this important mission, Ju-52 No. 1 completed seven flights. Its crew consisted of Senior Lieutenant Ioan Rată, first pilot; Lieutenant Constantin Crețu, second pilot; Major Ioan Eisae, observer; Lieutenant Ion Vulpe, radio operator; and Senior Technician Ion Ilie, aircraft mechanic.

They completed the flights at an altitude of 4,000 metres, each lasting between four and five hours, under the best possible conditions. The missions required great precision, with errors of more than 100 metres in altitude or five degrees in direction not permitted.

On the route Craiova–Caransebeș–Șerbulești–Caransebeș, the crew encountered exceptionally difficult weather, flying through snow clouds, but managed the task very well.

Eventually, new Li-2 aircraft were supplied from the Soviet Union for military aviation, while An-2s were acquired for sporting aviation. The Ju-52s were finally withdrawn, bringing to an end the service of aircraft whose exceptional longevity continues to attract the attention of aviation specialists today.

It appears that 1955 was their final year of service in Romania. In any case, we are inclined to believe that they could have continued flying for longer, and it would have been good if at least one had been preserved so that people today could admire a truly successful transport aircraft.
