## Martin B-57G Canberra "Tropic Moon III"

### The grandfather of all modern strike fighters

In June 1969, 16 B-57Bs were sent to the Martin plant in Baltimore
where they were to undergo a major refit under a dual contract with
Westinghouse. A bulbous radome and an ungainly chin fairing were added
to the Canberra. These housed forward-looking radar as well as the business
ends of the IR and LLLT (low light level television) cameras and an early laser rangefinder that
was used in this case for targeting newly developed LGBs. Aesthetically,
the B-57G was not about to win any prizes, but somehow the ugly look
went with the sinister nature of its business. After deployment to the
combat zone, crews flying the aircraft wore a patch showing a three-headed
dragon with white, red and green eyes, each of which signified a separate
weapons system. The last Canberras left the theatre on 12 April 1972, along with the rest
of the 13th BS, thus ending the Canberra's long operational tenure in Southeast Asia. Of the 16 B-57Gs
made, 11 were sent into combat but only one was lost. The remaining five were used in the US for training
and weapons system testing and development

The B-57Gs were subsequently assigned to the 13th BS
'Grim Reapers', which had been reactivated in February 1970. In
September of that year the unit returned to Southeast Asia, where it was
based at Ubon RTAB under the command of Lt Col Paul R Pitt as part
of the predominantly F-4 Phantom II-equipped 8th TFW. The unit's
new mounts no longer boasted guns in the wings, but they did continue
to carry the old, but still effective, incendiary cluster 'funny bombs'
internally, as well as four 260-lb fragmentation bombs or 500-lb Paveway
I LGBs under the wings.

WSO Capt. R. K. Markel says: 'The sensor range was limited. Often, by the time a target was spotted,
we were past bomb release point, and I could not find the target when we
returned. The Moving Target Indicator (MTI) was a dead loss - this was
an ongoing problem with the aeroplane that was never solved. The ECM
gear was nice, but unnecessary. If there were any radar-directed weapons
in the parts of Laos that we patrolled, none of them ever lined up on any
of the aircraft in which I flew. I was told sensor maintenance was difficult,
but other than the MTI, in 140 missions I never had a sensor fail. Indeed,
I never arrived at the jet on the ramp
to find it was not ready because of
an aircraft or equipment problem.
Furthermore, I was told that the
LGBs had a failure rate of three
percent. I bettered that average.
Every LGB I dropped guided
successfully. Our groundcrews
were absolutely fantastic. Whatever
shortcomings our equipment had,
those people were up to taking care
of it.'

## Summary

Specialized **night truck-hunters**, built around finding and attacking moving vehicles on the Ho Chi Minh Trail.

- Period: October 1970 onward
- Primary unit: 13th Bomb Squadron (Tactical), 8th Tactical Bombardment Wing
- Initial operating base: Ubon Royal Thai Air Force Base, Thailand
- Main area: Laos, particularly the Ho Chi Minh Trail
- Operation: Initially Commando Hunt V

Sixteen B-57Bs were converted into B-57Gs under Tropic Moon III. The aircraft received a completely redesigned nose containing a suite of night-attack sensors:

* AN/APQ-139 moving-target-indicator radar — detected and tracked moving vehicles.
* LLLTV — low-light-level television.
* FLIR — forward-looking infrared.
* Laser rangefinder/designator — supplied precise range information and allowed employment of laser-guided weapons.
* Digital computer system for target tracking and weapons delivery.
* Radar warning/ECM equipment and additional crew protection. ([Defense Media][1])

The basic mission was:

> **Find trucks moving along the Trail at night → identify and track them with the sensors → attack them with bombs, preferably precision-guided weapons.**

The aircraft generally operated at relatively low altitude over the target area and could loiter while searching, rather than simply making a conventional high-speed bombing run. The radar was particularly important because it could detect moving vehicles even when visual conditions were poor. ([Defense Media][2])

### What a typical mission looked like

A B-57G would depart Ubon at night, head toward its assigned section of the Trail in Laos, and begin searching for traffic. Once the sensor operator detected a potential target, the aircraft could use the different sensors together:

**Radar → locate moving vehicle/convoy**
↓
**LLLTV / FLIR → identify and confirm target**
↓
**Laser system → determine range/designate target**
↓
**Computer → calculate weapon delivery**
↓
**B-57G → attack**

The main attraction was the ability to do this **at night**, when North Vietnamese truck traffic was deliberately concentrated to reduce exposure to American air power. The B-57G could carry conventional ordnance, but the introduction of Paveway I laser-guided bombs was particularly important. The aircraft could use its own laser equipment to designate targets, giving it a considerably more precise attack capability than the earlier B-57s. ([Defense Media][1])

The aircraft wasn't invulnerable. During the initial combat evaluation, B-57G 53-3906 (the aircraft lost on 12/13 December 1970) was operating near Tchepone when its crew repeatedly made passes to mark a convoy for F-4s. North Vietnamese anti-aircraft fire eventually hit the Canberra and forced the crew to abandon it. Lt Col Paul R. Pitt and Lt Col Edwin A. Buschette both parachuted out and were rescued the following day. The wreckage was subsequently bombed to prevent the North Vietnamese from recovering the sophisticated sensor equipment. ([Defense Media][2])

### How effective was it?

The B-57G was a genuinely sophisticated system for 1970, but it had an important problem: the AC-130 gunships were even better suited to sustained truck hunting. During Commando Hunt V the B-57Gs claimed substantial numbers of trucks destroyed, but comparisons of kills per sortie showed the side-firing gunships had a much higher effectiveness in this particular mission. That increasingly pushed the USAF toward the AC-130 concept rather than continuing to develop the B-57G as the principal Trail interdiction aircraft. ([Wikipedia][3]) There was even an attempt to turn a B-57G into a gunship under Project Pave Gat, fitting an M61 20-mm cannon in the bomb bay. It demonstrated that the concept could work, but it wasn't adopted operationally—the B-57G's days were already numbered. ([Wikipedia][4])

### In the end...

Tropic Moon III wasn't itself a single mission or operation. It was the aircraft/sensor development programme that produced the B-57G. The actual combat missions were principally night interdiction missions under Commando Hunt, hunting road traffic in Laos. The initial combat evaluation ran from mid-October 1970 to 17 January 1971, with eleven B-57Gs involved. The B-57Gs subsequently continued operating in Southeast Asia until their withdrawal in April/May 1972. ([Defense Media][2]) Many consider the program a failure, but I reckon it was an excellent prototype of the current strike fighters, a successful test bed for modern air power.
