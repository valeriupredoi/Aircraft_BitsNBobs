F-4B **152219, NE-102**, of VF-21 *Freelancers*, was one of the aircraft involved in the first confirmed US Navy MiG kills of the Vietnam War, on 17 June 1965. And there's a fascinating detail: Batson and Doremus's engagement was more complicated than simply firing a Sparrow and watching a MiG fall.

**Aircraft and crew**

- Aircraft: McDonnell Douglas F-4B Phantom II
- Bureau number: 152219
- Modex: NE-102
- Squadron: VF-21 *Freelancers*
- Carrier: USS Midway (CVA-41)
- Pilot: Lieutenant Jack E. D. Batson Jr.
- RIO: Lieutenant Commander Robert B. Doremus
- Date: 17 June 1965
- Victim: North Vietnamese MiG-17 Fresco

This was part of a two-Phantom interception which also involved Cdr Louis C. Page and LCDR John C. Smith in NE-101. Both crews were credited with a MiG-17 victory.

## **1. The mission: 17 June 1965**

The two Phantoms (NE-101 BuNo 150646, and their wingman NE-102 BuNo 152219) were providing MiGCAP cover for a major strike against the Thanh Hoa Bridge and nearby targets in North Vietnam. The strike launched from Midway at approximately 09:00. After refuelling from a KA-3B tanker, the two-ship VF-21 section headed north. Batson and Doremus were flying NE-102. Interestingly, the aircraft carried the names of Page and Smith on its canopy rails, while NE-101 carried the names of VF-21 CO Bill Franke and his RIO James Mills. The pair orbited at approximately 10,000 feet, looking for approaching enemy aircraft. Initially, the radar contacts were around 50 nautical miles away over Hanoi. They weren't considered an immediate threat. Once the strike aircraft completed their mission and headed south, Page decided to make one more northbound pass. That decision brought them into contact with four MiG-17s.

## **2. The interception**

[usni.org](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/2026/january/red-sea-operations-offer-wrong-lessons-pacific?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The engagement took place at approximately 10:30, around 40 miles south of Hanoi. Page and Smith detected the targets, with Batson and Doremus following their lead. The Phantoms accelerated to approximately 500 knots. The four MiGs were approaching at roughly 400 knots The combined closure rate was enormous: more than 1,000 knots. Their pre-planned tactics were particularly interesting. They had worked out a forward-quarter interception:

- The lead Phantom would visually identify the MiGs.
- The wingman would maintain separation and launch a radar-guided AIM-7 Sparrow.
- The lead would manoeuvre vertically after visual identification.

This was designed around the restrictive rules of engagement requiring positive visual identification before opening fire. As the aircraft approached, Batson spotted four small specks at approximately ten miles. Page transmitted:

**"It's MiGs!"**

The MiGs turned towards the Phantoms, and the engagement began.

## **3. The Sparrow shots**

Page fired first. Batson watched the missile's detonation and initially thought it had missed. Then he saw the MiG's right wing separate. Meanwhile, Doremus was working the radar interception solution. The MiG that Batson and Doremus were tracking attempted a wingover manoeuvre. Doremus called the necessary corrections:

- Pull up!
- Fly the dot!
- Push over!
- Go down!

At approximately three miles, Batson obtained the correct firing solution and launched his AIM-7. The missile accelerated away from NE-102. Batson maintained radar illumination, then selected afterburner and pulled up as planned. When he looked for Page, the other Phantom was already above him. They regrouped and returned to the engagement area. Both crews could see missile smoke trails, and Smith spotted a parachute. Two MiGs had been destroyed.

## **4. The return to Midway**

Fuel was becoming a serious consideration. Batson reported that he was below bingo fuel and the two Phantoms began their return. Midway offered a tanker, but Page declined. Batson eventually landed with just 500 pounds of fuel remaining. There were no celebratory victory passes. This was a fairly serious fuel situation! Back aboard the carrier, the crews were greeted enthusiastically. Doremus turned to Batson and remarked:

**"Four more to go!"**

A reference to the four additional victories required to become an ace. There was also an amusing historical coincidence: Secretary of the Navy Paul Nitze happened to be visiting Midway that day. He was able to congratulate Page personally. The crews subsequently flew their aircraft to Tan Son Nhut for a press briefing. The Navy's first confirmed MiG kills of the Vietnam War had become front-page news!

## **5. The fascinating third MiG**

For years, the engagement was recorded as two MiG-17 victories. However, declassified documentation from 1997 reportedly established a third MiG loss. Batson had maintained that a third MiG was destroyed after ingesting debris from its wingman following the explosion of his Sparrow. The third victory was subsequently credited to Batson and Doremus. Dave Baranek discusses this in his excellent HistoryNet article, drawing on interviews with Batson and Michael O'Connor's research. There is, however, a discrepancy worth knowing: the USS Midway historical website still lists this third victory as probable rather than confirmed. The official Navy historical victory list records the two crew victories, not a separate third entry.

## **6. What happened to the crew afterwards?**

**Jack E. D. Batson Jr.** continued his naval service through the deployment and subsequently left the Navy, pursuing an engineering career with Western Electric.

**Robert B. Doremus** - nn 24 August 1965, he was shot down while flying with VF-21 CO Bill Franke. Initially declared killed in action, both men were subsequently discovered to have survived and been captured. Doremus spent seven and a half years as a prisoner of war, finally returning home on 12 February 1973. He retired from the Navy as a captain in 1986. Doremus also received the Silver Star for the 17 June engagement. His citation specifically credits him with one confirmed MiG kill and helping secure the second by diverting the remaining enemy aircraft away from the American strike force.&#x20;

**The Aircraft: 152219**

152219 crashed in 1967:

- Date: February 12, 1967
- Incident: While flying an aircraft intercept training mission over the Gulf of Tonkin / South China Sea from the USS Enterprise (CVA-65), the aircraft entered a cloud formation on a downward trajectory and suffered pilot disorientation.
- Casualties: Radio contact was lost and the aircraft ditched or crashed into the sea; both crew members—Lieutenant Commander Martin Joseph Sullivan and Lieutenant (j.g.) Paul Carlson—were killed.

**152219 as NE-101**

This article <https://theaviationgeekclub.com/correcting-the-record-on-first-confirmed-mig-killing-phantom-of-the-vietnam-war/amp/?utm_source=chatgpt.com> explains a long lasting confusion that on their MiG-killing mission of Jun. 17, 1965, Commander Lou Page and Lieutenant JC Smith were flying F-4B BuNo 150646 — not 151488 as sometimes reported. It also documents the fact that 150646 was lost on Jul. 28, 1965, just a few weeks after the MiG kill. The loss of 150646 is detailed in the <www.vietnamairlosses.com> database: The aircraft suffered engine and electrical power failure; both crewmen ejected and were rescued. Consequently, 152219 was given the NE-101 modex, and this is how it is shown in the only photo I could find of her from 1965, as NE-101, sometime during Midway's Dixie Station operations, August-October 1965.
