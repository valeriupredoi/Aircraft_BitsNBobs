## SEPECAT Jaguar GR.1A XZ118

Jaguar **XZ118** is one of the more famous RAF SEPECAT Jaguar airframes because it had an unusually colourful operational career followed by an even more unusual retirement.

Here are the highlights:

* **Construction number:** S.119
* **First flight:** 12 May 1976
* **Delivered to the RAF:** 4 June 1976 as a **Jaguar GR.1**, later upgraded to **GR.1A** and finally **GR.3A**

### Gulf War fame

During **Operation Granby** (the UK's contribution to Desert Storm), XZ118 served with No. 41 Squadron RAF.

It became famous for carrying one of the RAF's best-known pieces of nose art:

> **"Buster Gonad and his Unfeasibly Large Testicles"**

The artwork was based on the character from the British comic *Viz*, and was painted by Cpl. Paul Robins. It was typical of the unofficial humour seen on many RAF Jaguars deployed to the Gulf in 1991. Some of the paintings were commissioned by the pilots, others were just emblazoned on the aircraft in the quieter moments of the operation; also they daubed some graffiti on to the bombs, but this was stopped as it was deemed offensive to the Iraqis and Arabs (which a 1000lb bombs is not of course). ### Operational career

XZ118 spent much of its service with **41 Squadron** at RAF Coltishall, serving through the Cold War and into the 2000s before retirement in 2005. It was one of the Jaguars upgraded with:

* GR.1A avionics (1986)
* Gulf War mods in 1990 (Desert Pink ARTF scheme, overwing Sidewinder rails, enhanced RWR, ALQ-101 ECM pod, other Granby modifications   before deployment to Oman and then the Gulf)
* later GR.3A standard (1998)
* more powerful Adour Mk 106 engines (2004)
* improved navigation and targeting systems

### Gulf War

During Operation Granby, XZ118 belonged to No. 41 Squadron RAF, based in peacetime at RAF Coltishall, Norfolk. Its deployment followed the typical pattern for the RAF Jaguar force:

* Home station: RAF Coltishall
* Unit: No. 41 Squadron (part of the Coltishall Jaguar Wing)
* Initial deployment (August 1990): Thumrait Air Base, Oman, where the Jaguars built up their operational capability and acclimatised.
* Operational wartime base (January–February 1991): Muharraq Airfield, Bahrain, from where the Jaguars flew combat sorties over Kuwait and Iraq.

One interesting detail is that although XZ118 remained a 41 Squadron aircraft, the Jaguar force in theatre was effectively run as a combined wing called the Jaguar detachement (JagDet). Aircraft and crews from 6, 41 and 54 Squadrons operated together from Al Muharraq, and maintenance personnel were pooled, so individual aircraft were not always flown exclusively by crews from their parent squadron; all-in-all JagDet flew 158 missions, almost one third of the RAF missions during Operation Granby, even if the Jaguars amounted to one sixth of the RAF combat aircraft deployed to the Gulf. XZ118 flew 38 combat missions from Muharraq during the air campaign, carrying out low-level strike and reconnaissance tasks against Iraqi targets, in a mission pattern described more like a "medium level dive bombing", a novel approach. It even launched an AIM-9L Sidewinder: the launch occurred during a mission when the aircraft's crew mistook a target/track as a possible Iraqi threat. The Sidewinder was launched, but it failed to achieve an air-to-air kill (the missile did not hit an Iraqi aircraft). The event became something of a famous Jaguar anecdote because it was unusual: the Jaguar was a strike aircraft carrying self-defence missiles, and actual Sidewinder employment by RAF Jaguars in combat was exceptionally rare.

### The world's most unusual preserved Jaguar

Instead of ending up in a museum in the normal way, XZ118 became part of an art installation by Fiona Banner.

The aircraft was displayed **upside down** at Tate Britain in 2010. Banner described it as lying "like a submissive animal," turning a Cold War strike aircraft into a commentary on power, conflict and display. The installation attracted considerable attention because visitors could walk beneath the aircraft and inspect parts of the underside that are normally impossible to see.
For Jaguar enthusiasts, XZ118 therefore has a unique legacy:

* a genuine Gulf War veteran,
* carrier of one of the RAF's most famous nose-art schemes,
* and probably the only Jaguar to become a major contemporary art exhibit.

XZ118 was scrapped shortly after the exhibition closed in early 2011.
